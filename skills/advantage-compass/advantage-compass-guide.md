# The Advantage Compass — Facilitator Guide

> Folder: `advantage-compass` · Category: self-assessment · solo · 25 min · paper or online · assessment-type
> License: CC BY 4.0

A personal strategy tool, not a personality test. The Advantage Compass crosses two axes every career decision already depends on — where you are strong, and what the future needs — and maps your work into four quadrants. One of them is your golden track: the work you should double down on. Two of them are traps with familiar names: firefighting, and burnout.

---

## Why another self-assessment

Most self-assessment stops at inventory: here are your strengths, here are your weaknesses, good luck. An inventory cannot answer the question that actually matters — *given my strengths, where should I spend the next year?*

The Compass borrows from corporate strategy, where this question was solved decades ago. SWOT analysis tells a company not just what it has, but where to commit resources. The Compass imports that move and rebuilds it for a person, with one deliberate upgrade: it adds **time** as an axis. Companies fight over market share; people fight over their calendar. The interesting conflicts — vision against today's fires, strength against the drag of weakness — only become visible when time is in the model.

The result is one page: four quadrants, each naming what your work there is worth and what it costs.

## The two axes

**Horizontal — Strength × Weakness.** What you do with low effort and high output, versus what drains you for average results. Self-ratings work here because you are the only witness to your effort: the question is not *am I good at this* but *what does this cost me*.

**Vertical — Vision × Pain.** Vision is the work you want to be doing in three years — the pull. Pain is today's problems, deadlines, and other people's emergencies — the push. The gap between them is where careers are decided.

The quadrants:

| Quadrant | Axes | Plain name | What it is worth |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Vision × Strength | The golden track | Compounds — every hour makes the next hour more valuable |
| 2 | Vision × Weakness | The learning zone | Worth exactly one investment: enough to unblock the golden track |
| 3 | Pain × Strength | The firefighting track | Efficient, praised, and worth less every month |
| 4 | Pain × Weakness | The burnout trap | Costs energy, produces mediocrity, proves nothing |

## How to run it

Four steps, about 25 minutes, paper or online.

**Step 1 — Collect evidence, not opinions (10 min).** List the last two weeks of work, hour by hour if you can. For each block, mark two things: how good you are at it (strong / weak) and who wanted it (your vision / today's pain). Opinions about yourself arrive pre-flattered; a calendar does not lie.

**Step 2 — Place the evidence (5 min).** Sort every block into a quadrant. Most people discover their actual calendar is 15% golden track, 60% firefighting, and the rest damage. That number is the assessment.

**Step 3 — Read the map (5 min).** Three questions, in order: Which single block in Q1 is worth protecting first? Which Q3 obligation could be delegated or automated within a month? Which Q4 commitment should be ended, not improved?

**Step 4 — Commit to one move (5 min).** One concrete change, dated, with a check-back date in two weeks. Not a life plan — one move. The Compass is re-run monthly; strategies are revised, not regretted.

## The known distortions

1. **The competence mirage.** You are good at Q3 work, so you keep doing it — being needed feels like being valued. The test is not *am I good at this* but *does this make next year easier or harder*. Firefighting is rewarded in the present and punished by the future.
2. **The humility discount.** Q1 strengths get under-marked because they feel effortless — *anyone could do this*. They could not. If something feels easy to you and valuable to others, it is a strength; the effortlessness is the signal, not the counterargument.
3. **The weakness project.** Q4 work gets defended as *growth*. Some is. The test: does this weakness block the golden track, or just bruise the ego? Only the blocking kind deserves training, and only up to "good enough" — minimum viable competence, not mastery.
4. **The vision confusion.** Pain is loud and vision is quiet; under deadline pressure, everything looks like Q3. If everything lands in one quadrant, the axes are being read wrong — go back to Step 1 and use the calendar, not the memory.

## Facilitator notes for one-to-one use

The Compass is self-scored, but it runs powerfully as a 25-minute coaching conversation: the coachee fills Steps 1–2 alone, then reads the map out loud while you ask only the three Step-3 questions. Do not counsel; ask. The most useful facilitator move is the silence after *does this make next year easier or harder* — people answer it honestly when nobody rescues them from the pause.

For teams: run it individually first, then aggregate the quadrants on a shared wall. A team whose calendar is 80% Q3 does not have a productivity problem; it has a strategy problem, and now it can see which one.

## Variations

- **The quarterly audit:** same tool, longer memory — three months of calendar instead of two weeks. Slower to run, sharper at exposing the firefighting ratchet.
- **The career crossroads version:** at a job decision, score the *offer* against the quadrants, not your calendar. A role that is 70% Q4 is a well-paid burnout; the salary is buying your Q1 hours.
- **The team allocation version:** managers map their reports' quadrants instead of their own. The honest question shifts from *who works hardest* to *who is working on the future*.
