How the two rounds run

  • Round A: the describer talks, the drawer draws — no questions allowed.
  • Round B: roles swap, new image — the drawer may ask exactly three questions.
  • Same pair, two systems. The gap between the rounds is the lesson.

Rules, said out loud before you start

  • The describer may not say what the image IS — only what it looks like.
  • Nobody turns around. The drawer holds the marker, not the map.
  • Thick marker, not pencil — mistakes should be visible.
  • Pair daily colleagues for round A; randomize the pairs for round B.

What the exercise exposes

  • Stories instead of shapes — “it’s like a farm…” tells the drawer nothing.
  • The silent guess: drawing something plausible instead of asking.
  • The cost of “I sent it, so they got it” — the reveal prices that belief.
WorkshopSkill · Blind Portrait — Slide Deck · CC BY 4.0

Three ways it fails, three rescues

  • Narrating the story, not the shapes → freeze ten seconds, repeat the rule: describe what you SEE.
  • The drawer goes abstract to avoid being wrong → do not fix it live; the debrief is built for this.
  • Pairs finish early and drift → hand them the judge card: score round A against round B.
WorkshopSkill · Blind Portrait — Slide Deck · CC BY 4.0

Three questions at the reveal

1. Round A’s gap: how much came from the rule, how much from the description?

2. Of the three questions you could ask in round B — which helped most?

3. Where in your week do you send instructions into silence?

The measure

  • Before pairing up: six rows about your own last week — the usual, not the game.
  • Right after the debrief: the same six rows, answered about this exercise.
  • The number to watch is the delta, not the score.

Take one habit out

  • Ask one clarifying question before you start — the drawer’s three questions are a rehearsal.
  • Fix the describing, not the drawer, when the gap surfaces.
